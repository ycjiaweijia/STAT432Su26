





## Bias-Variance Tradeoff

A central idea in statistical learning is the **bias-variance tradeoff**.

To see the mathematical form, suppose the true data-generating model is

$$
Y=f(X)+\varepsilon,
$$

where

$$
\Exp[\varepsilon]=0,
\qquad
\Var(\varepsilon)=\sigma^2.
$$

After fitting a model to a training set, we obtain an estimator $\hat f$. If we predict at a new point $x_0$, the test mean squared error is

$$
\Exp\left[
\left(Y_0-\hat f(x_0)\right)^2
\right],
$$

where

$$
Y_0=f(x_0)+\varepsilon_0.
$$

The expectation is over both the randomness in the training data used to fit $\hat f$ and the new noise $\varepsilon_0$.

Add and subtract $\Exp[\hat f(x_0)]$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Exp\left[
\left(Y_0-\hat f(x_0)\right)^2
\right]
&=
\Exp\left[
\left(
f(x_0)+\varepsilon_0-\hat f(x_0)
\right)^2
\right] \\
&=
\Exp\left[
\left(
f(x_0)-\hat f(x_0)
\right)^2
\right]
+
\sigma^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

Now write

$$
f(x_0)-\hat f(x_0)
=
\left\{f(x_0)-\Exp[\hat f(x_0)]\right\}
+
\left\{\Exp[\hat f(x_0)]-\hat f(x_0)\right\}.
$$

The cross term has expectation zero, so

$$
\Exp\left[
\left(
f(x_0)-\hat f(x_0)
\right)^2
\right]
=
\left(
f(x_0)-\Exp[\hat f(x_0)]
\right)^2
+
\Var(\hat f(x_0)).
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
\Exp\left[
\left(Y_0-\hat f(x_0)\right)^2
\right]
=
\operatorname{Bias}^2(\hat f(x_0))
+
\operatorname{Var}(\hat f(x_0))
+
\sigma^2
}
$$

where

$$
\operatorname{Bias}(\hat f(x_0))
=
\Exp[\hat f(x_0)]-f(x_0),
$$

and

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\hat f(x_0))
=
\Exp\left[
\left(
\hat f(x_0)-\Exp[\hat f(x_0)]
\right)^2
\right].
$$

The three terms have different meanings:

- **bias squared** measures systematic error from using a model that is too simple or structurally wrong;
- **variance** measures sensitivity to the particular training sample;
- **irreducible error** $\sigma^2$ is noise in the response that cannot be removed by any model using $X$.

::: {.callout-note}
## Irreducible error

Even if we knew the true function $f$, the new response would still be

$$
Y_0=f(x_0)+\varepsilon_0.
$$

The noise term $\varepsilon_0$ creates error that prediction methods cannot eliminate.
:::

## What Is Statistical Learning?

Statistical learning studies how to use data to understand relationships and make predictions.

We usually model the relationship between predictors $X$ and response $Y$ as
$$
Y = f(X) + \varepsilon
$$

where:

- $X$ represents predictors
- $Y$ represents the response
- $f$ is the underlying relationship
- $\varepsilon$ represents random noise or unexplained variation

The main objective of statistical learning is to estimate the unknown function $f$ using observed data.


## Main Types of Learning


### Supervised Learning

- We observe both predictors $X$ and response $Y$.
- Goal is to predict $Y$ from $X$.

Examples:
- house price prediction
- spam detection
- medical diagnosis

### Unsupervised Learning

- We observe only predictors $X$, without labels $Y$.
- Goal is to discover hidden structure in the data

Examples:
- clustering
- dimensionality reduction
- anomaly detection

### Reinforcement Learning

- An agent interacts with an environment and learns through rewards and penalties.

Examples:

- robotics,
- game playing,
- recommendation systems.



### Regression and Classification

Within supervised learning, two major tasks are:

- **regression**, where the response is quantitative;
- **classification**, where the response is categorical.

For example:

- predicting salary is a regression problem;
- predicting whether a tumor is benign or malignant is a classification problem.



## Different Types of Data

Machine learning methods are strongly connected to data structure, and different data types often require different modeling strategies. In this course, we mainly focus on tabular data because it is the most classical setting in statistics and applied machine learning. However, it is still useful to briefly discuss several major data types and their associated modeling ideas.

### Tabular Data

Tabular data is the classical setting in statistics and machine learning.

| Age | Income | Balance | Student | Default |
|---:|---:|---:|:---:|:---:|
| 23 | 50000 | 1200 | Yes | No |
| 45 | 90000 | 3000 | No | Yes |

In tabular data:

- rows represent observations,
- columns represent variables or features.

Mathematically,

$$
X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p},
$$

where:

- $n$ is the number of observations,
- $p$ is the number of predictors.



Examples:
- finance,
- healthcare,
- insurance,
- marketing,
- scientific experiments.

::: {.callout-note}
## Main Idea

The key feature of tabular data is not simply its matrix representation. More importantly, each column corresponds to a specific variable with its own semantic meaning, such as age, income, blood pressure, or transaction count. This differs from other data types that will be discussed later, such as image data, where nearby pixels mainly encode spatial structure, or time series data, where temporal ordering plays a central role.
:::




### Image Data

Image data contains spatial structure, meaning that nearby pixels are often highly related. Modern image models frequently use:

- convolutional neural networks,
- vision transformers.

A central goal in image analysis is to extract meaningful visual patterns such as edges, textures, shapes, and objects from the image.

### Text Data

Text data contains sequential and contextual structure. The meaning of a word often depends heavily on surrounding words and broader context. Modern NLP is largely based on:

- transformers,
- large language models.


### Time Series Data

Time series data is ordered over time.

Examples:

- stock prices,
- weather data,
- sensor measurements.

Important ideas include:

- trend,
- seasonality,
- temporal dependence.


### Graph Data

Graph data represents relationships between objects.

Examples:

- social networks,
- citation networks,
- molecular structures.

Modern graph learning often uses graph neural networks.


### This Course Focuses on Tabular Data

This course mainly focuses on tabular data because it remains one of the most important data types in applied statistics and machine learning.

For tabular prediction problems, two model families are especially important:

1. linear models;
2. tree-based models.

These two families form much of the foundation of modern machine learning for structured data.







## Main Questions in Statistical Learning

At a high level, statistical learning is concerned with one central problem:

> How can we learn useful patterns from data?

This broad problem leads to several different kinds of questions.


### Prediction

> What will happen for a new observation?

Prediction is often the primary goal in modern machine learning. The focus is not just fitting the observed data, but making accurate predictions on new data.

Examples:

- Will a customer default?
- What will tomorrow's demand be?
- Will a patient develop a disease?

Mathematically, prediction is often viewed as estimating

$$
\hat f(x) \approx \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X=x].
$$

In practice, prediction mainly focuses on predictive performance.

### Generalization

> Will the prediction rule work well on unseen data?

Generalization is the key issue behind prediction. A model that performs well only on training data but poorly on new data is usually not useful.

This leads to several central ideas:

- training error versus test error,
- overfitting and underfitting,
- cross-validation,
- bias--variance tradeoff,
- model complexity.

Model complexity is closely related to generalization. Roughly speaking:

- Simple models:
  - higher bias,
  - lower variance.
- Flexible models:
  - lower bias,
  - higher variance.

As model flexibility increases:

- training error usually decreases,
- test error may eventually increase due to overfitting.

A central challenge in statistical learning is choosing a level of model complexity that gives good generalization performance.

### Inference and Understanding

> Which variables matter, and how are they related to the response?

Inference is about understanding relationships, not only making predictions.

Examples:

- Does smoking increase disease risk?
- Which features drive customer churn?
- Which predictors are statistically significant?

Common tools include:

- standard errors,
- confidence intervals,
- hypothesis testing.

Inference usually relies more heavily on model assumptions and diagnostics.


### Causality

> What happens if we intervene?

Causal inference goes beyond prediction and association. It asks what would happen if we actively changed one variable.

A causal question is often written as

$$
\mathbb{E}[Y \mid do(X=x)].
$$

Causal inference requires additional assumptions or experimental design. Examples include:

- randomized experiments,
- instrumental variables,
- difference-in-differences.



::: {.callout-note}
## Summary of the Distinctions

| Task | Main Question |
|---|---|
| Prediction | What will happen? |
| Generalization | Will it work on new data? |
| Inference | Is there a relationship? |
| Causality | What happens if we intervene? |


Important points:

1. Good training performance does not imply good generalization.
2. Good prediction does not imply valid inference.
3. Association does not imply causation.

:::


## Bias--Variance Tradeoff

A central idea in statistical learning is the **bias--variance tradeoff**.

Suppose

$$
Y=f(X)+\varepsilon,
\qquad
\Var(\varepsilon)=\sigma^2.
$$

For a fitted estimator $\hat f$, the test mean squared error at $x_0$ satisfies

$$
\mathbb{E}
\left[
(Y_0-\hat f(x_0))^2
\right]
=
\operatorname{Bias}^2(\hat f(x_0))
+
\operatorname{Var}(\hat f(x_0))
+
\sigma^2.
$$

The three terms represent:

- **bias**:
  systematic modeling error;

- **variance**:
  sensitivity to the training sample;

- **irreducible error**:
  noise that cannot be removed.

Roughly speaking:

- Simple models:
  - higher bias,
  - lower variance.

- Flexible models:
  - lower bias,
  - higher variance.

As model flexibility increases:
- training error usually decreases,
- test error often first decreases and then increases.

The goal is to balance underfitting and overfitting in order to achieve good generalization performance.

::: {.callout-note}
## Main Point

Many modern methods can be viewed as ways to control the bias--variance tradeoff. Regularization, cross-validation, ensemble methods, trees, boosting, and neural networks can all be understood from this perspective.
:::

# Classical Statistics vs Modern Machine Learning

Historically:

| Classical Statistics | Modern Machine Learning |
|---|---|
| inference | prediction |
| interpretable models | predictive performance |
| smaller datasets | large datasets |
| stronger assumptions | weaker assumptions |

Modern statistical learning often combines ideas from both areas.





## What Is Statistical Learning?

Statistical learning studies how to use data to understand relationships and make predictions. In most applications, we observe a response variable $Y$ and a set of predictors $X = (X_1, \dots, X_p)$, and we want to learn how $Y$ depends on $X$.

A common abstract representation is

$$
Y = f(X) + \varepsilon,
$$

where $f$ is the systematic part of the relationship and $\varepsilon$ is random noise.

The main goals are usually:

- **prediction**: accurately predict the response for new observations;
- **inference or explanation**: understand how the predictors are related to the response.


## Supervised and Unsupervised Learning

In **supervised learning**, we observe both predictors and a response. The goal is to predict or explain the response.

Examples include:

- predicting house prices from size, location, and age;
- predicting whether an email is spam from its content;
- predicting a patient's risk from clinical measurements.

In **unsupervised learning**, we observe predictors but no response. The goal is to discover structure in the data.

Examples include:

- clustering similar observations;
- reducing dimension with principal component analysis;
- finding latent groups or patterns.


## Regression and Classification

Within supervised learning, two major tasks are:

- **regression**, where the response is quantitative;
- **classification**, where the response is categorical.

For example:

- predicting salary is a regression problem;
- predicting whether a tumor is benign or malignant is a classification problem.


## Prediction Versus Inference

Prediction and inference are related, but they are not the same.

If the goal is **prediction**, we care mainly about how well the model performs on new data. A more complex model may be acceptable if it improves predictive accuracy.

If the goal is **inference**, we care about understanding the role of each predictor. In this case, interpretability is often more important than pure predictive performance.

::: {.callout-note}
## Main Point

The best predictive model is not always the best explanatory model. Statistical learning often requires choosing between interpretability and flexibility.
:::


## Parametric and Nonparametric Methods

A **parametric method** assumes a specific functional form for $f$. For example, linear regression assumes that

$$
f(X) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \cdots + \beta_p X_p.
$$

This assumption simplifies estimation and interpretation, but it may be too restrictive.

A **nonparametric method** does not assume a fixed functional form. It is more flexible, but it usually requires more data and may be harder to interpret.

Thus:

- parametric models are simpler and easier to interpret;
- nonparametric models are more flexible but may have higher variance.


## Training Error and Test Error

When we fit a model, we can measure how well it matches the observed data. This is the **training error**.

However, what really matters is performance on new observations. This is the **test error**.

A model can have very small training error but still perform poorly on new data. This phenomenon is called **overfitting**.

- **Underfitting** occurs when the model is too simple to capture the signal.
- **Overfitting** occurs when the model is too flexible and starts fitting noise.

Good statistical learning aims to minimize test error, not merely training error.


A simple model tends to have high bias but low variance. For example, fitting a straight line to a strongly nonlinear relationship creates systematic error, but the fitted line may be stable across training samples.

A flexible model tends to have lower bias but higher variance. For example, a very deep tree may adapt to nonlinear structure, but small changes in the training data can produce a very different tree.

As model flexibility increases:

- training error usually decreases;
- test error often decreases at first, then increases once overfitting begins.

The goal is to find a level of complexity that balances bias and variance well.

This gives the usual U-shaped test-error curve:

| Model behavior | Bias | Variance | Typical result |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Too simple | high | low | underfitting |
| Balanced | moderate | moderate | good generalization |
| Too flexible | low | high | overfitting |

::: {.callout-important}
## Why This Matters

Many modern methods can be understood as ways to manage the bias-variance tradeoff. Regularization, cross-validation, and model comparison are all tools for this purpose.
:::


## A Typical Statistical Learning Workflow

A practical workflow often looks like this:

1. Define the prediction or explanation goal.
2. Explore and preprocess the data.
3. Choose one or more candidate models.
4. Estimate model performance using validation or cross-validation.
5. Compare models and select an appropriate final model.
6. Interpret results and communicate conclusions.

This course will revisit this workflow repeatedly with different methods.


## Looking Ahead

The early chapters focus on linear regression and its extensions. Later chapters introduce more flexible models, resampling methods, regularization, and practical machine learning workflow.

The broad progression is:

1. start with simple interpretable models;
2. allow more flexible structure when needed;
3. evaluate models using principled estimates of test error;
4. improve prediction through regularization and modern methods.

## Prediction, Inference, and Causality

In statistics and data science, **prediction**, **statistical inference**, and **causal inference** are three fundamental tasks.  
They are **not hierarchical**, but they differ in goals, assumptions, and methodologies.

---

### 1. Prediction

**Goal:** Accurately predict the outcome $Y$ given predictors $X$.

$$
\hat f(x) \approx \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X = x]
$$

- Focus: **out-of-sample performance** (test error, cross-validation)
- Assumptions: relatively weak
- Methods: Ridge, Lasso, trees, boosting, neural networks

**Key idea:**  
Prediction captures patterns in the data and is validated empirically by generalization.

---

### 2. Statistical Inference

**Goal:** Draw conclusions about relationships or parameters in the population.

Examples:
- Is $\beta_j = 0$?
- What is the confidence interval for $\beta_j$?

- Focus: **uncertainty quantification**
- Requires: **model assumptions** (linearity, independence, etc.)
- Tools: standard errors, p-values, confidence intervals

**Key idea:**  
Inference generalizes from sample to population under specified assumptions.

---

### 3. Causal Inference

**Goal:** Determine the effect of interventions.

$$
\mathbb{E}[Y \mid do(X = x)]
$$

- Question: *What happens if we change $X$?*
- Requires: **causal structure or experimental design**
  - Randomized experiments (RCT)
  - Instrumental variables (IV)
  - Difference-in-differences (DiD)

**Key idea:**  
Causal inference identifies how the data-generating mechanism responds to interventions.

---

### 4. Relationship and Differences

Although distinct, the three tasks can be viewed as requiring **increasing levels of structure and information**:

| Task        | Required Information        | Validation Method        | Question Answered        |
|------------|----------------------------|--------------------------|--------------------------|
| Prediction | Data distribution          | Test error / CV          | What will happen?        |
| Inference  | Model + assumptions        | Theory + diagnostics     | Is there a relationship? |
| Causality  | Structure + identification | Design + assumptions     | What if we intervene?    |

---

### 5. Why Causality Is Harder

Causal inference is more difficult because:

1. **Data alone is insufficient**

   $$
   P(Y, X) \neq P(Y \mid do(X))
   $$

2. **Confounding variables** may distort relationships

3. **Counterfactuals are unobservable**

   We cannot observe both outcomes under different interventions for the same unit.

---

### 6. Interaction Between the Three

- Predictive models can help identify useful variables or nonlinear structure.
- Inferential models provide interpretable summaries and uncertainty quantification.
- Causal analysis requires additional assumptions beyond both prediction and inference.

**Important:**
> Good prediction does not imply valid inference or causal conclusions.  
> Significant associations do not imply causation.

---

### 7. Summary

> Prediction, inference, and causality are complementary but fundamentally different statistical tasks.  
> Each requires its own assumptions, validation criteria, and methodology.

**One-line intuition:**
- Prediction: *What will happen?*  
- Inference: *Is there a relationship?*  
- Causality: *What happens if we intervene?*

**Final takeaway:**
> Prediction is validated by data, inference is justified by assumptions, and causality requires design.

# Precision, Recall, Hypothesis Testing, and the Bias-Variance Tradeoff

## Introduction

In many introductory machine learning courses, precision and recall are introduced as simple formulas computed from a confusion matrix. However, this approach often hides the deeper statistical meaning behind these quantities.

Precision and recall are fundamentally connected to:

- statistical decision theory
- hypothesis testing
- asymmetric loss
- uncertainty
- threshold selection
- the bias-variance tradeoff

To fully understand classification, we must recognize an important fact:

> Classification is not merely prediction.
> It is decision making under uncertainty.

A classifier does not directly observe the truth. Instead, it observes noisy predictors and attempts to make decisions about class membership. Different types of mistakes may have very different consequences.

This is where precision and recall become important.

---

# Binary Classification Setup

Suppose we have a binary classification problem.

The true class is:

$$
Y \in \{0,1\}
$$

where:

- $Y=1$ represents the positive class
- $Y=0$ represents the negative class

The classifier produces predictions:

$$
\hat Y \in \{0,1\}.
$$

The outcomes may be summarized using the confusion matrix.

| | Predicted Positive | Predicted Negative |
|---|---|---|
| True Positive Class | TP | FN |
| True Negative Class | FP | TN |

where:

- TP = true positives
- FP = false positives
- FN = false negatives
- TN = true negatives

---

# Accuracy and Its Limitations

The most obvious performance measure is accuracy:

$$
\text{Accuracy}
=
\frac{TP+TN}{N}.
$$

Accuracy measures the proportion of correct predictions.

At first glance, this seems reasonable. However, accuracy implicitly assumes:

> all mistakes have equal importance.

In practice, this assumption is often unrealistic.

For example:

- missing cancer may be much worse than a false alarm
- falsely accusing an innocent person may be worse than missing a criminal
- incorrectly flagging legitimate transactions may frustrate customers

Thus many classification problems involve asymmetric decision costs.

This motivates precision and recall.

---

# Recall

Recall is defined as:

$$
\text{Recall}
=
\frac{TP}{TP+FN}.
$$

Recall measures:

> Among all truly positive observations,
> how many were successfully detected?

Equivalently:

:contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}

Thus recall is a conditional probability.

It measures how sensitive the classifier is to positive observations.

For this reason, recall is also called:

- sensitivity
- true positive rate

---

# Statistical Interpretation of Recall

Recall is fundamentally related to:

```text
detection ability
```

A classifier with high recall rarely misses true positive observations.

Low recall means:

```text
many false negatives
```

In statistical language, recall is closely connected to:

```text
Type II error
```

or equivalently:

```text
statistical power
```

since:

$$
\text{Power}
=
1-\text{Type II Error}.
$$

Thus recall is essentially the probability of correctly detecting a true signal.

---

# Hypothesis Testing Interpretation of Recall

Suppose we formulate classification as hypothesis testing.

For each observation:

$$
H_0: Y=0
$$

versus

$$
H_1: Y=1.
$$

Then:

- predicting positive corresponds to rejecting $H_0$
- predicting negative corresponds to failing to reject $H_0$

Under this interpretation:

| Classification | Hypothesis Testing |
|---|---|
| False positive | Type I error |
| False negative | Type II error |

Thus recall becomes:

$$
P(\text{Reject }H_0 \mid H_1 \text{ true}),
$$

which is precisely statistical power.

This is one of the deepest statistical interpretations of recall.

---

# Precision

Precision is defined as:

$$
\text{Precision}
=
\frac{TP}{TP+FP}.
$$

Precision measures:

> Among all predicted positive observations,
> how many are truly positive?

Equivalently:

:contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

Notice that this conditional probability is reversed compared with recall.

Recall conditions on the truth.

Precision conditions on the prediction.

This distinction is extremely important.

---

# Statistical Interpretation of Precision

Precision measures:

```text
trustworthiness of positive predictions
```

High precision means:

- few false positives
- positive predictions are reliable

Low precision means:

- many false alarms
- many predicted positives are actually negative

Thus precision is closely related to:

```text
false discovery control
```

and:

```text
positive predictive value
```

in medical statistics.

---

# Precision and Bayes' Rule

Precision and recall are connected through Bayes' theorem.

Recall is:

$$
P(\hat Y=1\mid Y=1).
$$

Precision is:

$$
P(Y=1\mid \hat Y=1).
$$

Bayes' rule gives:

Thus precision depends not only on recall, but also on:

- class prevalence
- prediction frequency

This explains why precision can collapse in highly imbalanced datasets.

---

# Class Imbalance

Suppose only 1% of observations are positive.

Even a classifier with high recall may have poor precision.

For example:

- 100 true positives
- 900 false positives

Then:

$$
\text{Recall}
=
1
$$

but:

$$
\text{Precision}
=
\frac{100}{1000}
=
0.1.
$$

Thus most predicted positives are incorrect.

This is why accuracy alone becomes misleading under class imbalance.

---

# Thresholds and Decision Rules

Most classifiers produce scores or probabilities:

$$
p(x)=P(Y=1\mid x).
$$

A final classification requires a threshold:

$$
\hat Y=
\begin{cases}
1 & p(x)>c\\
0 & p(x)\le c
\end{cases}
$$

where $c$ is the classification threshold.

Changing the threshold changes precision and recall.

---

# Precision-Recall Tradeoff

Suppose we lower the threshold.

Then the classifier predicts positive more aggressively.

This usually causes:

- recall to increase
- precision to decrease

because:

- more true positives are detected
- but more false positives are also generated

Conversely, raising the threshold produces:

- higher precision
- lower recall

Thus precision and recall naturally trade off against each other.

---

# Statistical Decision Theory

This tradeoff is fundamentally a decision-theoretic problem.

Classification is not merely about finding the truth.

Instead, it is about:

> making decisions under uncertainty while balancing different error costs.

Different applications prioritize different errors.

---

# Medical Screening Example

Suppose we are screening for cancer.

Missing a cancer patient may be extremely costly.

Thus false negatives are dangerous.

Therefore we prioritize:

```text
high recall
```

Even if this creates more false positives.

Extra medical testing may be acceptable if it reduces missed diagnoses.

---

# Spam Filtering Example

Now consider spam detection.

If precision is low, many legitimate emails will be flagged as spam.

Users may become frustrated.

Thus precision becomes more important.

This illustrates that the optimal tradeoff depends on the application.

---

# Precision, Recall, and the Bias-Variance Tradeoff

Precision and recall are also connected to the bias-variance tradeoff.

Suppose a classifier is extremely conservative.

It predicts positive only when highly certain.

Then:

- precision may be high
- recall may be low

This resembles a high-bias classifier.

The classifier avoids risky decisions.

---

# High Variance and Aggressive Classification

Now suppose the classifier aggressively predicts positives.

Then:

- recall may increase
- precision may decrease

because the classifier begins fitting more subtle patterns.

This resembles lower bias but higher variance.

The classifier becomes more flexible and sensitive, but also more unstable.

---

# Thresholds as Complexity Control

Changing the classification threshold can itself be viewed as controlling effective model complexity.

A very low threshold creates:

```text
aggressive positive prediction
```

A very high threshold creates:

```text
conservative positive prediction
```

Thus threshold selection acts like a bias-variance tuning mechanism.

---

# Example: Logistic Regression

Suppose logistic regression estimates:

$$
P(Y=1\mid x).
$$

If we classify positive whenever:

$$
P(Y=1\mid x)>0.5,
$$

then the classifier behaves moderately.

If we instead use:

$$
P(Y=1\mid x)>0.1,
$$

the classifier becomes far more aggressive.

This usually increases recall but decreases precision.

---

# Precision-Recall Curves

A precision-recall curve shows how precision changes as recall changes across thresholds.

This curve is especially important for imbalanced datasets.

Unlike ROC curves, precision-recall curves directly reflect the quality of positive predictions.

---

# F1 Score

Sometimes we want to balance precision and recall simultaneously.

The F1 score is:

$$
F1
=
\frac{2PR}{P+R},
$$

where:

- $P$ = precision
- $R$ = recall

This is the harmonic mean of precision and recall.

---

# Why Harmonic Mean?

The harmonic mean heavily penalizes imbalance.

For example:

- precision = 1
- recall = 0

then:

$$
F1=0.
$$

Thus F1 favors classifiers that balance both quantities.

---

# Precision, Recall, and Model Flexibility

Different models naturally produce different precision-recall behaviors.

Highly flexible models:

- deeper trees
- boosting
- neural networks

may achieve higher recall by detecting subtle patterns.

However, they may also generate more false positives.

Simpler models:

- linear models
- regularized models

may produce fewer false positives but miss weaker signals.

Thus precision-recall behavior is deeply tied to model complexity.

---

# Boosting and Recall

Boosting methods such as AdaBoost and XGBoost often aggressively improve recall.

Boosting repeatedly focuses on difficult observations.

As a result, boosting tends to:

- reduce false negatives
- improve detection power

However, aggressive boosting may also increase false positives if the model begins fitting noise.

Thus boosting often navigates a delicate precision-recall tradeoff.

---

# Hypothesis Testing Analogy

The connection between classification and hypothesis testing is profound.

Classification threshold selection resembles choosing a significance level.

For example:

- lower threshold
  - easier to reject $H_0$
  - higher recall
  - more false positives

- higher threshold
  - harder to reject $H_0$
  - lower recall
  - fewer false positives

This parallels:

- liberal tests
- conservative tests

in classical hypothesis testing.

---

# Unified Statistical View

We may summarize the connections as follows.

| Classification | Hypothesis Testing |
|---|---|
| Positive prediction | Reject $H_0$ |
| Negative prediction | Fail to reject $H_0$ |
| False positive | Type I error |
| False negative | Type II error |
| Recall | Power |
| Precision | Reliability of discoveries |

This unified viewpoint connects machine learning classification directly to classical statistics.

---

# Final Remarks

Precision and recall are not merely machine learning metrics.

They arise naturally from:

- statistical decision theory
- hypothesis testing
- uncertainty quantification
- asymmetric loss
- bias-variance tradeoffs

Recall measures the ability to detect true signals.

Precision measures the reliability of positive discoveries.

Different applications require different balances between these quantities.

Ultimately, classification is not simply about maximizing accuracy.

It is about making statistically meaningful decisions under uncertainty while balancing competing risks. -->

In regression, the main goal is often to predict a continuous response accurately, and in many cases RSS is good enough to evaluate the model. In classification, things become much more complicated.


Classification is not only about predicting a label. In many applications, the model first produces a score or probability, and then a decision rule converts that information into an action. As a result, different evaluation metrics measure different aspects of model performance.

Classification evaluation can be divided into three related but distinct goals.



- Decision quality focuses on the final hard classifications obtained after applying a threshold to predicted scores or probabilities. This perspective focuses on whether the model makes useful decisions in practice. Metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score all belong to this category.
- Ranking quality evaluates whether positive observations tend to receive higher scores than negative observations. This perspective is important when the exact threshold is not fixed or when we mainly care about prioritization. ROC curves, AUC, and precision--recall curves are commonly used to evaluate ranking performance.
- Probability quality evaluates whether predicted probabilities are numerically trustworthy. For example, among all observations predicted to have probability near $0.8$, we expect roughly $80%$ of them to truly belong to the positive class if the model is well calibrated. Calibration curves, Brier score, and log loss are commonly used to evaluate probability quality.

It can be summarized into the following table. 

| Goal | Main question | Common tools |
|---|---|---|
| Decision quality | Are the hard classifications useful? | Accuracy, precision, recall, F1 |
| Ranking quality | Are positives ranked above negatives? | ROC curve, AUC, PR curve |
| Probability quality | Are predicted probabilities trustworthy? | Calibration curve, Brier score, log loss |

These goals are not identical.

- A model may achieve high accuracy but poor recall.
- A model may have strong ranking performance (high AUC) but poorly calibrated probabilities.
- A model may produce well-calibrated probabilities while still making poor classification decisions under a particular threshold.


::: {.callout-note}
In practice, classification evaluation can become quite sophisticated and may involve ranking performance, probability calibration, decision costs, and threshold optimization.

However, in this course, our primary goal is to understand the core ideas of statistical learning and predictive modeling. Therefore, we will mainly focus on accuracy and closely related classification metrics.
:::

## Bias-Variance in Classification Evaluation

The bias-variance tradeoff also appears in classification, but it is easiest to state for probability estimation.

Suppose the true conditional probability is

$$
p(x)=P(Y=1\mid X=x),
$$

and a fitted model estimates this probability by

$$
\hat p(x).
$$

At a fixed point $x_0$, the Brier loss is squared error for a binary outcome:

$$
\left(Y_0-\hat p(x_0)\right)^2.
$$

Since

$$
Y_0\mid X=x_0\sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(p(x_0)),
$$

the conditional variance of $Y_0$ is

$$
\Var(Y_0\mid X=x_0)
=
p(x_0)(1-p(x_0)).
$$

The expected Brier loss decomposes as

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Exp\left[
\left(Y_0-\hat p(x_0)\right)^2
\right]
&=
\Exp\left[
\left(p(x_0)-\hat p(x_0)\right)^2
\right]
+
p(x_0)(1-p(x_0)) \\
&=
\operatorname{Bias}^2(\hat p(x_0))
+
\operatorname{Var}(\hat p(x_0))
+
p(x_0)(1-p(x_0)).
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus, for probability estimation:

- bias means the model systematically estimates probabilities too high or too low;
- variance means the estimated probabilities change a lot across training samples;
- $p(x_0)(1-p(x_0))$ is irreducible Bernoulli noise.

This is why probability-quality metrics such as Brier score and log loss are closely connected to the bias-variance tradeoff.

Flexible classifiers, such as deep trees and random forests, may reduce bias by capturing nonlinear structure, but their raw probability estimates may have higher variance or poor calibration. Simpler or regularized models may have higher bias but more stable probability estimates.

::: {.callout-note}
## Thresholds and bias-variance

Changing the classification threshold does not change $\hat p(x)$ itself. It changes the decision rule built on top of $\hat p(x)$.

So threshold tuning trades off false positives and false negatives. Model tuning trades off bias and variance in the fitted scores or probabilities.

### Reliability Diagram

A calibration curve, also called a reliability diagram, is built as follows:

1. Group observations into bins based on predicted probability.
2. For each bin, compute the average predicted probability.
3. For each bin, compute the observed positive frequency.

=
\text{predicted probability}.
$$

If the curve lies below the diagonal, the model is overconfident. For example, when it predicts $0.9$, the true frequency may only be $0.7$.

If the curve lies above the diagonal, the model is underconfident. For example, when it predicts $0.3$, the true frequency may be $0.5$.

The Brier score is a proper scoring rule for predicted probabilities:

$$
\text{Brier}
=
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n
\left(y_i-\hat p_i\right)^2.
$$

Lower Brier score is better.

Unlike accuracy, the Brier score evaluates the probability itself. A confident wrong prediction receives a large penalty.

### Log Loss

Another common proper scoring rule is log loss:

$$
\text{LogLoss}
=
-
\frac1n
\sum_{i=1}^n
\left[

+
(1-y_i)\log(1-\hat p_i)
\right].
$$

Log loss strongly penalizes confident wrong predictions.

Both Brier score and log loss evaluate probability quality, not only hard classification decisions.

## Calibration Methods

If a model produces useful rankings but poor probabilities, we can sometimes calibrate its scores.

Two common calibration methods are:

- Platt scaling,
- isotonic regression.

### Platt Scaling

Platt scaling fits a logistic regression model using the original classifier score as the predictor.

If the original model produces a score $s(x)$, Platt scaling estimates

$$
P(Y=1\mid s(x)).
$$

This learns a sigmoid transformation of the scores.

### Isotonic Regression

Isotonic regression learns a monotone transformation of the scores.

It is more flexible than Platt scaling because it does not assume a sigmoid shape. However, it can overfit when the calibration set is small.

Calibration should be learned on data not used to fit the original model.

# Classification Decisions and Evaluation

Unlike regression—where metrics such as Mean Squared Error (MSE) evaluate prediction error along a continuous scale—classification evaluation is fundamentally more nuanced.

A modern statistical learning classifier often produces a score or estimated probability rather than directly outputting a categorical decision. For example, a model may estimate

$$
\hat p(x)=\Pr(Y=1\mid X=x),
$$

and a separate decision rule then converts this soft prediction into a hard classification. As a result, classification performance can be evaluated from several different perspectives.

Broadly speaking, classification evaluation can be divided into three related but distinct goals.

| Goal | Main question | Common tools |
|---|---|---|
| Decision quality | Are the final classification decisions good? | Accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score |
| Ranking quality | Are positive observations ranked above negative observations? | ROC curve, AUC, PR curve |
| Probability quality | Are predicted probabilities numerically trustworthy? | Calibration curve, Brier score, log loss |

These goals are closely related, but they measure fundamentally different aspects of model performance. In statistical learning theory, these goals form a strict, top-down information hierarchy: Good probability estimates often lead to good ranking performance, and good ranking performance can often produce good classification decisions after thresholding. However, the converse implications generally fail.

Conceptually, this hierarchy may be summarized as

$$\text{Optimal Probabilities} \implies \text{Optimal Ranking} \implies \text{Optimal Decisions}$$

If a model accurately estimates the true conditional probability distribution $P(Y\mid X)$, then it naturally induces good rankings and supports effective decision-making across many possible thresholds or cost functions. In contrast, optimizing only a threshold-based metric such as accuracy provides no guarantee that the underlying probabilities are calibrated or that the ranking behavior is reliable.

In practice, however, decision quality is often easier to achieve and is frequently the primary objective in applied statistical learning. Many real-world applications ultimately require concrete actions, such as approving a loan, detecting fraud, or diagnosing disease. As a result, threshold-based metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score often play the central role in applied machine learning.

::: {.callout-note}
Classification evaluation can become quite sophisticated and may involve ranking performance, probability calibration, cost-sensitive learning, threshold optimization, and decision theory.

In this course, however, our primary goal is to understand the core ideas of statistical learning and predictive modeling rather than to develop a complete theory of statistical decision making. Therefore, we will mainly focus on accuracy and closely related threshold-based classification metrics.
:::
