7  Classification Decisions and Evaluation

7.1 Introduction

In statistical learning, classification is fundamentally different from ordinary regression.

In regression, the goal is usually to estimate a continuous response accurately.

In classification, however, the goal is not merely prediction. Instead, classification involves:

  • uncertainty
  • decision making
  • asymmetric error costs
  • signal detection
  • threshold selection

This makes classification closely connected to:

  • statistical decision theory
  • hypothesis testing
  • detection theory
  • the bias-variance tradeoff

Metrics such as precision, recall, ROC curves, and AUC are not merely machine learning formulas. They arise naturally from statistical ideas about uncertainty and decision making.

This chapter develops a unified statistical understanding of classification evaluation.

8 Binary Classification Setup

Suppose the response variable is binary:

\[ Y \in \{0,1\}. \]

where:

  • \(Y=1\) denotes the positive class
  • \(Y=0\) denotes the negative class

A classifier produces predictions:

\[ \hat Y \in \{0,1\}. \]

The outcomes can be summarized using the confusion matrix.

Predicted Positive Predicted Negative
True Positive Class TP FN
True Negative Class FP TN

where:

  • TP = true positives
  • FP = false positives
  • FN = false negatives
  • TN = true negatives

9 Accuracy

The most basic classification metric is accuracy:

\[ \text{Accuracy} = \frac{TP+TN}{N}. \]

Accuracy measures the proportion of correctly classified observations.

Although accuracy is intuitive, it has an important limitation:

It implicitly assumes all mistakes have equal cost.

In practice, this assumption is often unrealistic.

For example:

  • missing cancer may be far worse than a false alarm
  • falsely flagging a legitimate bank transaction may inconvenience customers
  • falsely accusing an innocent person may have severe consequences

Thus classification evaluation often requires more refined metrics.

10 Recall

Recall is defined as:

\[ \text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP+FN}. \]

Recall measures:

Among all truly positive observations, how many were successfully detected?

Recall is therefore a conditional probability.

It is also called:

  • sensitivity
  • true positive rate (TPR)

11 Statistical Interpretation of Recall

Recall measures the ability to detect true signals.

High recall means:

few false negatives

Low recall means:

many missed signals

Recall is closely related to:

Type II error

and:

statistical power

since:

\[ \text{Power} = 1-\text{Type II Error}. \]

Thus recall may be interpreted as:

the probability of correctly detecting a true signal.

12 Precision

Precision is defined as:

\[ \text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP+FP}. \]

Precision measures:

Among all predicted positive observations, how many are truly positive?

Equivalently:

:contentReferenceoaicite:1

Notice that the conditioning direction differs from recall.

Recall conditions on the truth.

Precision conditions on the prediction.

This distinction is extremely important.

13 Statistical Interpretation of Precision

Precision measures:

trustworthiness of positive predictions

High precision means:

  • few false positives
  • reliable positive discoveries

Low precision means:

  • many false alarms
  • many predicted positives are incorrect

Precision is closely related to:

  • false discovery control
  • positive predictive value

in statistics and medical testing.

14 Bayes’ Rule Connection

Precision and recall are connected through Bayes’ theorem.

Recall is:

\[ P(\hat Y=1\mid Y=1). \]

Precision is:

\[ P(Y=1\mid \hat Y=1). \]

Thus precision depends not only on recall, but also on:

  • class prevalence
  • prediction frequency

This explains why precision may collapse in highly imbalanced datasets.

15 Class Imbalance

Suppose only 1% of observations are positive.

Then even a classifier with excellent recall may have poor precision.

For example:

  • 100 true positives
  • 900 false positives

Then:

\[ \text{Recall}=1 \]

but:

\[ \text{Precision} = \frac{100}{1000} = 0.1. \]

Thus most predicted positives are incorrect.

This is why accuracy alone becomes misleading under severe class imbalance.

16 Classification Thresholds

Many classifiers output probabilities or scores:

\[ p(x)=P(Y=1\mid x). \]

To make a final decision, we choose a threshold:

\[ \hat Y= \begin{cases} 1 & p(x)>c\\ 0 & p(x)\le c \end{cases} \]

where \(c\) is the classification threshold.

Changing the threshold changes classification behavior.

17 Precision-Recall Tradeoff

Suppose we lower the threshold.

Then the classifier predicts positive more aggressively.

This usually causes:

  • recall to increase
  • precision to decrease

because:

  • more true positives are detected
  • but more false positives are also produced

Conversely, raising the threshold generally causes:

  • higher precision
  • lower recall

Thus precision and recall naturally trade off against each other.

18 Statistical Decision Theory

This tradeoff arises from statistical decision theory.

Classification is fundamentally:

decision making under uncertainty.

Different applications impose different error costs.

The optimal threshold therefore depends on the application.

19 Medical Screening Example

Suppose we are screening for cancer.

Missing a cancer patient may be extremely costly.

Thus false negatives are dangerous.

Therefore we prioritize:

high recall

even if this produces additional false positives.

Extra medical testing may be acceptable if it reduces missed diagnoses.

20 Spam Filtering Example

Now consider spam filtering.

If precision is low, many legitimate emails will be classified as spam.

Users may become frustrated.

Thus precision becomes more important.

21 Hypothesis Testing Interpretation

Classification may also be interpreted through hypothesis testing.

Suppose:

\[ H_0:Y=0 \]

versus

\[ H_1:Y=1. \]

Then:

  • predicting positive corresponds to rejecting \(H_0\)
  • predicting negative corresponds to failing to reject \(H_0\)

Under this interpretation:

Classification Hypothesis Testing
False positive Type I error
False negative Type II error
Recall Statistical power

Thus recall is essentially the power of a statistical test.

22 Thresholds and Significance Levels

Classification thresholds resemble significance levels in hypothesis testing.

Lower thresholds correspond to:

more aggressive rejection

This increases:

  • recall
  • false positives

Similarly, higher significance levels in hypothesis testing increase rejection frequency.

Conversely, conservative thresholds reduce false positives but may miss true signals.

Thus classification thresholds and significance levels play similar statistical roles.

23 ROC Curves

ROC stands for:

Receiver Operating Characteristic

An ROC curve plots:

  • true positive rate (TPR)
  • versus false positive rate (FPR)

across all thresholds.

The true positive rate is:

\[ TPR = \frac{TP}{TP+FN}. \]

which is recall.

The false positive rate is:

\[ FPR = \frac{FP}{FP+TN}. \]

Equivalently:

24 ROC Curves and Hypothesis Testing

ROC curves are deeply connected to hypothesis testing.

Recall that:

  • TPR corresponds to power
  • FPR corresponds to Type I error

Thus an ROC curve essentially studies:

power versus Type I error

across all thresholds.

This is one of the deepest statistical interpretations of ROC analysis.

25 Interpreting ROC Curves

A classifier near the diagonal line behaves almost randomly.

Better classifiers push the ROC curve toward the upper-left corner.

The ideal classifier would achieve:

  • TPR = 1
  • FPR = 0

meaning:

  • perfect detection
  • no false alarms

26 AUC

The area under the ROC curve is called AUC.

AUC summarizes ranking performance across all thresholds.

  • \(s(X^+)\) is the score of a random positive observation
  • \(s(X^-)\) is the score of a random negative observation

Thus AUC measures:

the probability that a random positive observation receives a higher score than a random negative observation.

This interpretation is fundamentally probabilistic and ranking-based.

27 Why AUC Is Threshold-Free

Accuracy depends on a specific threshold.

AUC does not.

Instead, AUC evaluates:

ranking quality across all thresholds

This makes AUC particularly useful when the optimal threshold is unknown.

28 Precision-Recall Curves

A precision-recall curve plots:

  • precision
  • versus recall

across thresholds.

PR curves are especially useful for imbalanced datasets.

29 ROC vs PR Curves

ROC curves may appear optimistic under severe class imbalance.

For example:

  • 99% negatives
  • very small FPR

may still generate many false positives.

PR curves reflect this problem more directly because they incorporate precision.

Thus PR curves are often preferred for:

rare-event detection

such as:

  • fraud detection
  • medical diagnosis
  • anomaly detection

30 F1 Score

The F1 score combines precision and recall:

\[ F1 = \frac{2PR}{P+R}. \]

where:

  • \(P\) = precision
  • \(R\) = recall

This is the harmonic mean of precision and recall.

31 Why Harmonic Mean?

The harmonic mean strongly penalizes imbalance.

For example:

  • precision = 1
  • recall = 0

then:

\[ F1=0. \]

Thus F1 favors classifiers that balance both quantities.

32 Precision, Recall, and the Bias-Variance Tradeoff

Precision and recall are also connected to the bias-variance tradeoff.

Suppose a classifier is extremely conservative.

It predicts positive only when highly certain.

Then:

  • precision may be high
  • recall may be low

This resembles a high-bias classifier.

The classifier avoids risky decisions.

33 Aggressive Classification and Variance

Now suppose the classifier aggressively predicts positives.

Then:

  • recall may increase
  • precision may decrease

because the classifier becomes more sensitive to subtle patterns.

This resembles lower bias but higher variance.

The classifier becomes more flexible but also more unstable.

34 Thresholds as Complexity Control

Changing the classification threshold may itself be viewed as controlling effective complexity.

A very low threshold produces:

aggressive positive prediction

A very high threshold produces:

conservative positive prediction

Thus threshold selection acts like a bias-variance tuning mechanism.

35 Model Complexity and Precision-Recall Behavior

Different model classes naturally produce different precision-recall behavior.

Highly flexible models:

  • deep trees
  • boosting
  • neural networks

may achieve higher recall by detecting subtle patterns.

However, they may also generate more false positives.

Simpler models:

  • linear models
  • regularized models

may achieve higher precision but lower recall.

Thus precision-recall behavior is closely tied to model complexity.

36 Boosting and Recall

Boosting methods such as AdaBoost and XGBoost often aggressively improve recall.

Boosting repeatedly focuses on difficult observations.

As a result, boosting tends to:

  • reduce false negatives
  • improve detection power

However, overly aggressive boosting may increase false positives if the model begins fitting noise.

Thus boosting often navigates a delicate precision-recall tradeoff.

37 A Unified Statistical View

We may summarize the relationships as follows.

Classification Statistical Interpretation
False positive Type I error
False negative Type II error
Recall Statistical power
Precision Reliability of discoveries
Threshold Significance cutoff
ROC curve Power vs Type I error
AUC Ranking probability
Threshold tuning Bias-variance tradeoff

38 Final Remarks

Classification evaluation is fundamentally statistical.

Metrics such as:

  • precision
  • recall
  • ROC curves
  • AUC
  • F1 score

all arise naturally from:

  • statistical decision theory
  • hypothesis testing
  • uncertainty quantification
  • asymmetric error costs
  • model complexity control

Recall measures the ability to detect true signals.

Precision measures the reliability of positive discoveries.

ROC curves study the tradeoff between detection power and false alarm rate.

AUC measures ranking quality across all thresholds.

Ultimately, classification is not merely about maximizing accuracy.

It is about making statistically meaningful decisions under uncertainty while balancing competing risks.
